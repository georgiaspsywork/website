## Why behaviour matters more than ever

Safety in the workplace has always been a priority, but modern work presents unique challenges. Long hours, shift patterns, hybrid teams and digital communication tools have changed how we interact and perform tasks. These changes bring new risks and new opportunities for safety improvements.

Consider this: how often do we overlook the subtle behaviours that lead to accidents or errors? Fatigue, miscommunication and stress can all contribute to unsafe conditions: HSE lists slower reactions, memory lapses, decreased awareness and underestimation of risk among the effects of fatigue (HSE, n.d.). Addressing these factors requires more than traditional safety protocols. It demands a deep dive into human factors and organisational psychology.

## What a human factors approach looks like

Human factors focuses on understanding and influencing the conditions that shape safety outcomes. It’s about identifying patterns, pressures and barriers that affect how people act in their work environment.

For example, an employee might know the safety rules but still take shortcuts due to time pressure, tiredness or unclear communication. Rather than asking why people didn’t follow the rule, a human factors approach asks what about the work made the shortcut the easy option, and works with leadership to redesign it.

This combines evidence-based strategies with a practical understanding of how the work really gets done, so that the people doing it, and the leaders around them, are part of the solution.

Key benefits include:

- **Solutions tailored** to the specific demands of your organisation
- **Real-time feedback and coaching** to reinforce positive behaviours
- **Data-driven insights** to track progress and identify areas for improvement
- **Approaches that scale** for small teams or large enterprises

By focusing on behaviour and work design rather than just compliance, organisations can reduce incidents, improve communication and boost overall performance.

## Practical steps to get started

Improving safety through human factors might seem daunting, but it can be broken down into manageable steps. Here’s a practical roadmap:

1. **Assess your current safety culture.** Use surveys, interviews and observations to understand existing behaviours and attitudes. Identify common risks, including fatigue, and areas where employees feel unsupported.
2. **Set clear objectives.** Define what success looks like. Is it fewer accidents, better reporting or improved teamwork? Clear goals help tailor the approach.
3. **Look at the work itself.** Review hours, workload, handovers and how tasks are organised, and use data to see where pressure builds.
4. **Engage leadership and employees.** Safety starts at the top. Ensure leaders are committed and visible in their support. Encourage employee participation through incentives and recognition. HSE also recommends consulting employees on working hours and shift patterns (HSE, n.d.).
5. **Deliver targeted training and coaching.** Use workshops, scenarios and coaching to make learning engaging and to address specific behaviours. Keep expectations realistic, though: employer programmes that rely mainly on education show weak evidence of lasting benefit (Redeker et al., 2019), so pair training with changes to the work itself.
6. **Monitor and adjust.** Regularly review data and feedback to refine the approach. Celebrate successes and address challenges promptly.

By following these steps, organisations can create a dynamic safety culture that adapts to changing needs and environments.

## Overcoming common challenges

Changing behaviour at work is not without its hurdles. Common challenges include:

- **Engagement:** keeping employees motivated can be tricky. Interactive, practical content helps maintain interest.
- **Access:** not everyone is reachable in the same way, particularly across shifts and sites. Offer flexible formats so no group is left out.
- **Measuring impact:** behaviour change is subtle and takes time. Combining quantitative data with qualitative feedback offers a fuller picture.
- **Maintaining momentum:** safety is an ongoing journey, not a one-time event. Regular updates, refreshers and leadership involvement keep it alive.

Addressing these challenges requires thoughtful planning and a commitment to continuous improvement. Remember, the goal is not just to tick boxes but to foster genuine behavioural change that enhances safety and wellbeing.

## Building a safer future with organisational psychology and human factors

At the heart of effective safety work lies a deep understanding of organisational psychology and human factors. These disciplines explore how people think, feel and interact within their work environment.

By applying these principles, organisations can:

- **Reduce employee fatigue** by designing workflows that respect natural rhythms and breaks. In one meta-analysis, injury risk was highest when rest breaks came less often than every four hours or lasted under 30 minutes (Fischer et al., 2017).
- **Improve communication** through clear, empathetic messaging and feedback loops. In a survey of critical care nurses, distractions, time stress and fatigue were each linked to poorer handover quality (Abou Hashish et al., 2023).
- **Boost decision-making** by creating supportive environments that encourage thoughtful risk assessment. Teams that feel safe to speak up about problems learn faster and perform better (Edmondson, 1999).
